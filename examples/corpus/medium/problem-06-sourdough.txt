Ash content for the type-550 wheat flour used in the trial sits at 0.54 percent, with falling number 312 s and a wet-gluten value of 29.8 percent. Particle-size distribution is centred on 85 micrometres; the tail above 150 micrometres was held below two percent. Water absorption in the farinograph reached 58.4 percent at 500 FU. These figures describe a flour intended for hearth loaves with moderate fermentation rather than for laminated pastry. Lot-to-lot variation should be checked against the certificate; blending to a target absorption is preferable to changing mix time ad hoc.

Storage at twelve percent moisture, cool and dark, keeps enzyme activity in a usable band. Flour older than ninety days in a warm bakery loft will show a drifting falling number even if the bag still looks clean. Do not assess sourness of a levain by colour. Titratable acidity and pH are the measurements that transfer between shifts. If absorption is lower than the certificate, increase water in small steps and record the farinogram, not the baker's mood. Specification sheets exist so the next bag does not become a new recipe.

One does not recover a nineteenth-century harbour loaf by copying a modern hydration percentage. Household books from those decades treat leaven as furniture: fed, scolded, carried to a neighbour when a house was sold. The sourness they praise is not our titration. It is a taste aligned with beer and with rye that had sat too long in a damp chest. To read those books as specifications is to miss that they are inventories of anxiety about waste. Protein contents were rarely stated; extraction rates were a matter of local pride and local cheating.

A baker who wants historical crumb is therefore chasing a moving target. One can still learn from the insistence on time. Doughs were not rushed because fuel was dear and ovens were not toys. The surviving advice is almost always "wait". That word has travelled better than any miller's mark. It will not appear on a certificate of ash content, and it will not save a loaf that was mixed from panic rather than from patience.
