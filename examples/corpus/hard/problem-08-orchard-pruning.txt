Stand back and assess the crown before cutting. Remove dead and diseased wood first. Cut to the collar; do not leave stubs. Do not climb with an unsecured saw. Where two leaders compete, retain one. Disinfect tools when moving from a cankered specimen to a healthy one. On mature apples, open the centre enough for light to reach fruiting wood that can be picked without a circus of ladders. Tie down overly vigorous laterals on young trees rather than removing them in haste.

Work in a sequence you can repeat: dead wood, crossing wood, height, then the tempting extra snips. Stop for a short rest after the first hour. Fatigue produces the cuts you will regret in July. Place prunings on the tarpaulin as you go; a tidy orchard is easier to inspect for missed stubs. Label a tree that needs a second visit rather than inventing a new plan in failing light. Record what you removed in a notebook the next person can read.

One should begin by regarding the tree as a system of light and load rather than as an obstacle. Dead wood is removed first, not because it is satisfying, though it is, but because it clarifies the remaining structure. Stubs are an invitation to decay. A saw that dangles from a climbing belt is an invitation of another kind. Competing leaders are a question of loyalty: one is kept. Tools are cleaned between a cankered tree and a sound one, a small rite that pays for itself in seasons one will not be present to witness.

On older apples it is reasonable to open the centre more generously than some manuals suggest, provided the remaining scaffold can bear a crop without splitting. A young branch that races upward may be tied rather than sacrificed. Fatigue is part of the orchard. One rests, or one begins to cut stories instead of wood. The notes one leaves should be legible to a stranger. Vanity pruning is still pruning, and it still shows in July.
