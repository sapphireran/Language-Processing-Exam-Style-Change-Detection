Surface sizing with diluted gelatine remains a standard intervention when iron-gall inscriptions sit on weakly sized nineteenth-century laid papers. The treatment aims to reduce aqueous mobility of residual sulphates during subsequent humidification, thereby limiting halo formation around the written line. Concentration, bloom strength, and application temperature jointly determine film continuity; excess solids produce a brittle surface that later fractures along the chain lines. Empirical tests on surplus endleaves indicate that a two-percent solution, applied by brush and blotted, offers a workable compromise between protection and reversibility.

However, gelatine is not a universal answer. Papers already possessing a heavy original size may cockle if rewetted, and parchment bindings adjacent to the text block can stain if the moisture front is poorly controlled. The literature therefore recommends local rather than overall application wherever the corrosion is confined to a few gatherings. Documentation should record solution batch, dwell time, and drying method, because later observers cannot reconstruct those choices from the sheet itself. These constraints, rather than any single recipe, constitute the conservation argument.
