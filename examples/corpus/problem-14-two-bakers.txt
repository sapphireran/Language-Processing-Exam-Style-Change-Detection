The rye prefers a long cool ferment; I give it twelve hours in the fridge and I do not rush the oven for guests.
I like the crumb a little wet so I hold back a spoon of flour even when the dough looks slack.
If the loaf sings I wait, because cutting early is how you get a gummy sermon about patience.
Steam for the first fifteen minutes, then dry heat, then I tap the base and I listen more than I look.
You should mix until the dough just clears the bowl, then stop, or you will build a tough crumb and call it rustic.
Keep the surface taut when you shape; slack shaping is the usual reason a rye spreads instead of lifting.
Proof only until a floured finger leaves a dent that slowly returns — fully returned means you waited too long.
I would rather underproof slightly than bake a loaf that collapses into a story about humidity.
Score the dough with one decisive stroke; hesitation is how a rye leaks sideways and then sulks.
