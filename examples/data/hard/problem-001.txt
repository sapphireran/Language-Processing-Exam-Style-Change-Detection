A starter that smells sharply of acetone is usually hungry rather than contaminated, although that distinction is easier to assert than to verify. Feeding at a higher inoculation ratio tends to shorten the time to peak and can reduce the harshness of the aroma. It is worth noting that flour type still dominates: a rye-heavy feed will ferment faster than an all-purpose feed at the same temperature. Many home bakers interpret early sourness as a virtue when it is often simply a sign of elapsed time.

Bulk fermentation is where most of the loaf's character is decided, yet it is also the stage most often watched by the clock instead of by the dough. A moderate increase in volume, a softer surface, and a few trapped bubbles at the edges are more reliable than a fixed number of hours. Cooler kitchens will of course stretch the schedule. The risk of overproofing rises quickly once the dough is shaped, so it is generally safer to stop the bulk a little early if the room is warm.

You will get a more open crumb if you keep the dough moving during bulk: a fold every thirty minutes for the first two hours is enough. Do not chase a huge rise in the bowl. Shape when the dough is airy but still has a little fight, then give it a cold overnight proof. Score with a confident stroke; a timid cut heals and the loaf finds its own weaker seam. Bake hotter than you think for the first fifteen minutes, then finish lower so the crust does not scorch.

If the sour flavor is taking over, shorten the cold proof or feed the starter more often in the days before you mix. Whole-grain flour will push acidity up even if your timing is tidy, so treat that as a recipe choice rather than a defect. Keep notes on room temperature. The same fold schedule will not behave in July the way it behaved in January.
