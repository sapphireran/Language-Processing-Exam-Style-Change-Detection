A neighbourhood walk is easy to describe as recreation and harder to describe as measurement. One useful discipline is to count sitting places that are actually in use, not merely installed. Another is to notice who disappears from the sidewalk when the sun leaves the frontages. These are ordinary observations, but they accumulate into an account of comfort that a traffic model will not generate on its own.

It is possible, of course, to over-read a single afternoon. A school-gate cluster can look like a permanent social density when it is only a twenty-minute pulse. Seasonal markets likewise inflate dwell counts. The safer habit is to walk the same block at mismatched hours and to treat the first vivid scene as a hypothesis. What remains after the third visit is usually the more honest map.

Walk the block once without a notebook and once with a strict tally: benches occupied, shade gaps, crossing waits, and doorways that invite lingering. Do the second pass at a different time of day. If a corner dies after dusk, write that down as a lighting and frontage problem, not as a mystery of character. The street already has a behaviour. Your job is to stop inventing a more flattering one.

Compare the tally with the official width standards. A generous sidewalk on paper can still fail if driveways interrupt it every few metres. Note the interruptions. Then decide whether the next intervention is shade, seating, or simply fewer curb cuts. Small corrections compound. Grand reconstructions are optional.
