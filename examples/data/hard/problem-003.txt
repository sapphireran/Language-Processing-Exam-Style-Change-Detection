Library late hours change the kind of work that gets done in a reading room. After nine the group projects thin out and the remaining bodies are mostly doing solitary revision. The air handling gets more noticeable. Chairs scrape less often. It is tempting to call this atmosphere, but it is also just a shift in who has a keycard and who has already gone home.

The same room at noon is a different instrument. Conversation leaks from the staircase. Laptops cluster around the few outlets that actually work. A person looking for quiet will misread the space if their only sample is an evening visit. Opening-hour policy is therefore not a courtesy; it is part of the room's function. Shorten the evening and you do not merely save staff cost. You delete a use.

There is a smaller effect that is easy to miss: late hours also change who feels entitled to stay. Students who commute long distances may never see the quiet version of the room. Local students harvest it. When a library advertises itself as a twenty-four-hour resource, the honest brochure would say for whom. Access that is theoretically open can still be socially scheduled.

Staffing models usually hide that distribution. A single security contractor after ten is not the same service as a desk that can answer a reference question. If the late room is only a warm place to sit, say so. Clarity is kinder than the rumour that the building is still a library when it has already become a shelter with desks.
