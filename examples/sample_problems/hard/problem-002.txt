The 7:14 usually arrives as a cluster of two cars and a rumour. I stand on the same paving slab because it lines up with a door that sometimes opens. People who commute every day invent these tiny superstitions and then defend them with a straight face. The heater works in one car and punishes you in the next. I read the same three pages of a book and never remember which three. When the train finally moves, the city looks briefly intentional.

On the 7:14 I still take the same slab. The door habit is silly and I keep it. One car is a furnace; the next is a fridge. I open the book, lose the page, close it. After the first lurch the windows show a city that almost has a plan.

Evening service is a different contract. The 18:40 is crowded in a way that makes politeness look like choreography: bags on laps, shoulders angled, nobody claiming the aisle as if they owned it. Announcements stack on top of each other until they become weather. I count stops because counting is cheaper than hoping. By the river the light goes theatrical for two minutes, and then we are back under warehouses, and then I am walking, and the day has a seam in it.
