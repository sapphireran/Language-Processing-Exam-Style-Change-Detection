A rain garden is a shallow planted basin that intercepts runoff from roofs and paved surfaces before the water reaches a storm drain. The soil mix is typically sand, compost, and native topsoil in proportions that drain within twenty-four to forty-eight hours. Therefore the designer sizes the basin from contributing area and local rainfall depth rather than from a decorative sketch. Overflow must still reach a safe outlet; the garden is a delay, not a dam. Moreover, plant choice should favor species that tolerate both standing water and later drought, because those two states will alternate.

You can dig a rain garden without making a science project out of it. Pick the low spot that already puddles, keep it a few metres from the foundation, and check that the water has somewhere to go when the bowl is full. Mix in compost if the dirt is tight clay. You'll plant wet-tolerant stuff in the bottom and drier stuff on the berm. Water it through the first season so the roots actually take. After that, you're mostly weeding and making sure the inlet isn't buried under leaves.

Siting remains the constraint that amateur installations most often miss. A basin too close to a basement wall can redirect moisture into the foundation drainage plane; a basin on compacted fill may perch water instead of infiltrating it. The contributing downspout should be screened, and the first-flush path should be inspected after heavy leaf fall. If infiltration tests show rates below a centimetre per hour, an underdrain is not an optional flourish. It is the difference between a garden and a mosquito trench.
